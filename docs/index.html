Delivery-Time Prediction

How long will this food delivery take, how sure are we, and what does a wrong ETA cost?

TL;DR. On 9,553 orders from the last ten days (never seen in training), gradient boosting predicts delivery time within 3.07 min on average (95% CI 3.02–3.11), 56% better than a city-and-distance lookup table. Only 0.9% of orders are off by 10+ minutes. Promising the model's 90th-percentile time instead of its point estimate cuts late-order refunds from $475 to $20 per 1,000 orders for 4.7 extra minutes on the promise.

Does the original random split overstate accuracy?

Barely. Random k-fold gives 3.06 min, folds grouped by city 3.08, and the time-ordered holdout 3.10 (1.4% worse than random). Every courier works in one city (53 per city on average), and the features describe conditions rather than places, so the model carries across cities and across weeks.

Data

43,862 delivery records from 22 cities, 2022-02-11 to 2022-04-06 (44 order days, 1,320 couriers). Public Uber Eats delivery-time dataset, MIT licence.

Method

Train on orders up to 2022-03-27, test on 2022-03-28 to 2022-04-06. Models: a lookup median by city type and distance band, ridge regression, and XGBoost with a 12-draw random search scored by city-grouped CV. 80% intervals come from two quantile gradient-boosting models (10th and 90th percentiles). Uncertainty on every headline number is a 1,000-draw bootstrap over holdout orders.

Limitations

  • Pickup delay is only known once the courier collects the order, so this is the ETA shown at pickup, not at checkout.
  • The interval covers 77.0% of orders against a 80% target; a conformal adjustment on a calibration week would close the gap.
  • All test couriers also appear in training (100%), so accuracy for new couriers is untested.
  • The refund amount and grace window are assumptions; costs scale linearly with the refund.
